Frequently asked questions
What license does a California pool builder need?
The Contractors State License Board's C-53 Swimming Pool license covers building and remodeling pools, including plastering. A B General Building contractor may take on a pool as part of a larger project. Every business in this directory holds a C-53 license, checked against CSLB records.
Does a new or remodeled pool need a permit and safety features?
Yes, a building permit, and in California a permit to build or remodel a pool or spa at a single-family home requires at least two of the seven drowning-prevention safety features listed in Health and Safety Code section 115922. Ask the builder which two are in the price.
How much can a pool builder ask for as a down payment?
On a California home improvement contract, no more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. Progress payments should not run ahead of the work actually done.
How are builders listed here?
Every contractor holding a C-53 license in the CSLB public files is listed, grouped by the city on its CSLB mailing address and sorted alphabetically. Nothing is rated or ranked, and every page shows the date of the data.
Do you charge builders to be listed?
No. No builder pays to appear, and the publisher receives nothing for any referral. The site is supported by Randy Beard's pre-contract review service for homeowners.
What does "suspended" mean on a listing?
CSLB has suspended the license, usually because a bond was cancelled, insurance lapsed or a judgment went unpaid. A suspended contractor may not contract for or perform work until CSLB reinstates the license. Check the license live on the day you sign.
What does "workers' comp exempt" mean?
The contractor has certified to CSLB that it has no employees. If a crew shows up to build your pool, ask whose employees they are and for proof of workers' compensation coverage.
Is the $25,000 contractor's bond enough protection?
Rarely. It is the legal minimum, and it is shared by everyone with a claim against that license. On a pool that costs ten times that, the contract is your real protection.
Why isn't a builder I know listed?

What's not in your pool contract
Most pool disputes are not about what the contract says. They are about what it leaves out. These are the items
Randy Beard sees missing most often in the contracts that end up in litigation, and the questions to ask before you sign.
Site and soil
Rock, hard digging and hauling. Many bids assume normal soil. Ask what happens, and at what price per hour or per load, if the crew hits rock, old concrete or fill.
Soils report and engineering. Hillside lots, expansive clay and fill often need a geotechnical report and engineered plans. Who orders it, who pays, and is the pool built to it?
Groundwater and dewatering. If the hole takes on water, pumping it out can run for weeks.
Access. Removing and replacing a fence, gate or block wall to get equipment into the yard, and repairing the lawn, sprinklers and driveway afterward.
Drainage. Where deck and roof water goes once the yard is covered in concrete.
Utilities
Electrical service. A new pool, heater and lights can exceed your panel. A panel upgrade or new subpanel is often "by owner."
Gas line. The run from the meter to the equipment pad, and whether the meter is big enough for a pool heater.
Relocating utilities in the way of the dig: sewer, water, irrigation lines, buried cable.
The pool itself
Allowances instead of prices. Tile, coping, plaster and decking listed as allowances. If you choose above the allowance, you pay the difference. Get the allowance amounts in writing.
Equipment by model number. "Pump, filter, heater" is not a specification. See which equipment should I get.
Deck area. The square footage included, and the price per extra square foot.
Plaster start-up. Brushing and water chemistry for the first weeks of new plaster, which decides whether it spots, streaks or discolors.
Automation, lighting, cleaner, cover. Often extras.
Permits, safety and paperwork
Permit and plan-check fees, and the cost of any corrections the city requires.
Drowning prevention features. California requires at least two of seven safety features (such as an enclosure, a safety cover, or alarms on doors to the pool) on a new pool at a home. Which two, and are they in the price?
Fencing and barriers the city requires to pass final inspection.
Warranty. What it covers (structure, plaster, equipment), for how long, and who honors the equipment warranty.
Change orders. California requires them in writing, signed, before the extra work. A contract that is vague here invites a large final bill.
Money and schedule
Down payment above $1,000 or 10 percent, whichever is less, is not allowed on a California home improvement contract.
Progress payments that run ahead of the work done.
Start and finish dates, and what happens if the job stalls.
The full list of what California requires in the contract itself is on the before-you-sign checklist.

Pool equipment: Pentair, Hayward or Jandy?
Pentair, Hayward and Jandy (part of Fluidra) make most of the residential pool equipment installed in California.
All three make good pumps, filters, heaters and automation. The brand matters less than four other things.
1. Sizing and plumbing matter more than the logo
An oversized pump on undersized pipe wastes power and wears out early. Ask the builder for the pipe sizes, the pump size and
the flow it is designed to deliver. California's energy code has specific rules for new residential pool plumbing and pumps,
including pipe sizing and limits on pressure loss through fittings.
2. Variable-speed pumps
Under federal efficiency rules and California's energy code, the filtration pump on a new pool is in practice a variable-speed
or multi-speed pump. It costs more up front and much less to run. If a bid lists a single-speed pump, ask why.
3. Heating: gas, heat pump, or both
California did not ban gas pool heaters. But under the state energy code (Title 24, section 110.4(c)) a gas heater by itself is
not one of the ways a new pool's heating system can meet the code. A common approach is a heat pump as the primary heater, with a
gas heater as a supplement. Ask your builder which path the plans use, and whether your gas meter and electrical service can carry it.
4. Who will service it
Pick the brand your local service companies stock parts for and know how to repair. A great heater is a bad choice if the
nearest technician who works on it is an hour away. Equipment warranties usually require licensed installation and may need registration.
What to get in writing
The make and model number of every piece of equipment: pump, filter, heater or heat pump, automation, lights, sanitizer, cleaner.
Pipe sizes, and the length of the run to the equipment pad.
Who registers the equipment warranties, and who you call when something fails in year two.
Bonding and grounding of the equipment, lights and metal around the pool, required by the electrical code and checked by the inspector.
Equipment by model number is one of the most common things left out of pool contracts.

Pool remodeling and replastering
A remodel is often a five- or six-figure job on a pool that already has problems. The interior finish, the tile line,
the coping and the equipment usually go at once, and sometimes the shell itself needs repair. These are the points Randy Beard
checks when a remodel goes wrong.
See licensed pool plastering and remodel contractors by county.
Replastering: preparation decides the outcome
New plaster over old plaster is where most bond failures happen. Ask how the old surface will be prepared: chipped out to the shell,
hydroblasted, or acid-washed and bond-coated, and why that method fits your pool.
Debonding, delamination and spalling are different failures with different causes. Get the finish named by product in the bid,
and keep the paperwork. Randy explains the difference in pool plaster delamination and surface defects.
Start-up. The first weeks of brushing and water chemistry decide whether new plaster spots, streaks or discolors. Who does it, for how long, and is it in the price?
Warranty. What the plaster warranty covers, and what it excludes as "water chemistry."
Cracks and structural repairs: the wheelbarrow problem
When a shell is cracked or a wall is moving, the usual fix is to jackhammer out the damaged section, drill in some new steel and hand-pack
concrete mixed in a wheelbarrow. Those patches often crack again within a year or two. Structural repair of a concrete pool shell has its own
standards, and a repair to a load-carrying wall may need engineering, a permit and inspection. Watch
The Wheelbarrow Problem: why concrete pool repairs fail.
Ask what caused the crack before you pay to cover it. Plaster over a moving crack will crack again.
Get the repair method in writing: who designed it, whether it needs a permit, and who inspects it.
Tile, coping and decks
Removing coping can damage the bond beam. Ask how it comes off and what happens if the beam is damaged.
Name the tile, coping and deck materials, and the square footage of deck included.
Equipment and electrical
A remodel is when equipment gets replaced. Get model numbers: see which pool equipment should I get.
New lights and equipment must be bonded and grounded to the current electrical code, which may mean work the old pool never had.
Permits and safety
A remodel can need a building permit. In California, a permit to build or remodel a pool or spa at a single-family home requires at least two
of the seven drowning-prevention safety features. Ask who pulls the permit and which features are in the bid.
Licensing
Replastering and remodeling a pool is pool work. The contractor who contracts with you for it should hold the C-53 Swimming Pool license.

===== NEW POOL BUYER QUESTIONS (approved answers, Randy Beard) =====
Q: How much does an inground pool cost in California?
A: It varies too much for a single number to mean anything. The size, the shape, a hillside or poor soil, access for equipment, the finish, the deck, the equipment and the city's permit fees can each move the price a lot. Get at least three written bids for the same scope, priced line by line, and compare what each one includes before you compare totals. If you'd like a second set of eyes on a bid, Randy reviews one residential bid for a flat $495.

Q: What costs usually aren't included in a pool bid?
A: The usual ones: hitting rock or old concrete in the dig, soils reports and engineering, pumping out groundwater, taking down and putting back a fence or wall for access, an electrical panel upgrade, the gas line run, and permit and plan-check fees. Watch for "allowances" for tile, coping, plaster and decking too. If you pick something above the allowance, you pay the difference. Ask every builder to put in writing what is excluded and the price of each likely extra.

Q: How do people usually pay for a pool?
A: Common routes are a home equity loan or line of credit, a cash-out refinance, a personal loan, or financing arranged through the pool company. Compare the rates and terms with your own lender before you sign anything the builder offers. Whatever the source of the money, California rules still apply: a small down payment, then progress payments tied to work actually done, never ahead of it. This is general information, not financial advice.

Q: Will a pool raise my home's value, and will my insurance go up?
A: Sometimes a pool helps a home sell, but it doesn't reliably return what it costs, and that depends a lot on the neighborhood. Homeowner's insurance premiums usually go up, and insurers often recommend more liability coverage, so call your agent before you build. A pool in poor shape can scare buyers off, so keep your permits, contract and service records.

Q: What does a pool cost to run each month?
A: It depends mostly on two things: the pump and the heat. A variable-speed pump sized to the plumbing costs much less to run than an oversized one. Heating is usually the biggest bill, whether that's a heat pump on electricity or a gas heater. Your local electric and gas rates matter, so ask the builder for the pump's design flow and the heater's size, and check them against your utility rates.

Q: How long does it take to build a pool in California?
A: Plan on months, not weeks. Design, engineering and the city's plan check alone can take several weeks, depending on the city. Construction then depends on the weather, inspections, the subcontractors' schedules and how much deck and landscaping work is involved. Make sure the contract states a start date and a completion date, and says what happens if the job stalls.

Q: What are the main stages of building a gunite pool?
A: Roughly: plans and permit, layout and excavation, steel, plumbing and electrical, then the gunite or shotcrete shell, which is inspected along the way. Next come tile and coping, the deck and the equipment, and the plaster or interior finish goes on last, followed by a careful start-up. Take photos at every stage, especially of the steel before the shell is shot, because those stages are covered up for good.

Q: What happens if it rains during construction, or during the winter after?
A: If your pool is being built going into the rainy season, ask the builder now what the rain plan is: a pump on site, a cover on the dirt pile, and a way to keep water out of open excavations. Once the pool is finished, don't drain it to "make room" for rain. A full pool's weight holds it down, and an empty shell in wet ground can crack or even lift. If overflow heads toward the house, lower the water a few inches, not feet, and keep the deck drains clear.

Q: What's the difference between gunite and shotcrete?
A: Both are concrete sprayed into place over steel to form the shell of most California inground pools. Gunite is the dry mix, with water added at the nozzle, and shotcrete is usually the wet mix. Either one works when it's applied right. What matters is good crew practice: removing rebound (the material that bounces off) instead of burying it in the shell, keeping enough concrete cover over the steel, and avoiding weak "cold joints" when work stops and starts.

Q: Should I get a gunite, fiberglass or vinyl pool?
A: Gunite or shotcrete (concrete) pools can be any shape or size and are the most common in California, but they need a good design for the soil and careful finish care. Fiberglass shells are factory-made, go in faster and come in set shapes and sizes, and vinyl liners cost less up front but the liner needs replacing every so often. Whichever you choose, the contractor who builds it should hold the C-53 Swimming Pool license, and the soil and site should drive the design.

Q: Do I need a soils report?
A: Often, yes, and on a hillside, on expansive clay or on fill it's usually essential. Standard pool plans often carry one set of details for ordinary soil and a heavier one for expansive soil. Without a soils report showing the ground is good, nobody should be picking the lighter design just because it's cheaper. A soils report costs a small fraction of the job, so ask who orders it, who pays, and whether the pool is built to it.

Q: What license does a California pool builder need?
A: The C-53 Swimming Pool license from the Contractors State License Board covers building and remodeling pools, including plastering. A B General Building contractor can take on a pool as part of a larger project. CSLB says a B or C-27 contractor contracting for a pool must hold the C-53 classification or subcontract the pool work to a licensed C-53 contractor. Every business in this directory holds a C-53, checked against CSLB records.

Q: What does "workers' comp exempt" mean on a listing?
A: It means the contractor told CSLB it has no employees. If a crew shows up to build your pool, ask whose employees they are and for proof of workers' compensation coverage. An uninsured worker hurt on your property can become your problem.

Q: What questions should I ask a pool builder before I hire them?
A: Ask for the license number and check it yourself. Ask for names of recent customers you can call, and what's excluded from the bid. Ask who pulls the permit and which two drowning-prevention safety features are in the price. Ask for every piece of equipment by make and model, what the warranty covers and who honors it, and who does the plaster start-up. Ask about the payment schedule too: it should be in dollars, tied to stages of work, never ahead of the work.

Q: Is the contractor's bond enough protection if something goes wrong?
A: Rarely. The $25,000 contractor's bond is the legal minimum, and everyone with a claim against that license shares it. On a pool that costs many times that, the contract is your real protection: keep your payments behind the work and collect lien releases as you pay.

Q: Can you tell me which builder is best?
A: No. This site doesn't rate or rank builders, no builder pays to be listed, and Randy receives nothing for referrals. What I can do is show you what the state's record says about a builder, and you can check the license yourself at cslb.ca.gov. Then get at least three written bids for the same scope and talk to each builder's recent customers.

Q: How much can a pool builder ask for as a down payment?
A: On a California home improvement contract, no more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. A bigger deposit request is a warning sign, so ask about it before you sign. This is general information, not legal advice.

Q: How should progress payments work, and when is the final payment due?
A: Payments should be listed in dollars and tied to stages of work, and no payment should run ahead of the work actually done. That's California law, and CSLB calls running ahead "frontloading." Hold the final payment until the plaster start-up is finished, the city's final inspection is signed off, and the equipment, decking and fencing in the contract are complete. Before that last check, run a garden hose on the new deck and make sure the water reaches the drains and comes out the other end.

Q: What does California require in a pool contract?
A: Among other things: the builder's name and license number matching CSLB, a description of the project with a plan and scale drawing of the pool, the down payment and progress payment sections, start and completion dates, change-order rules, the mechanics lien warning, and the notice of cancellation. Both of you sign and date it before work starts, and you get a completely filled-in copy. The rules are in Business and Professions Code sections 7159, 7159.5 and 7165 to 7168, and a pool contract that leaves required parts out can be unenforceable by the contractor. This is a practical checklist, not legal advice.

Q: Can I cancel a pool contract after I sign it?
A: A California home improvement contract has to include a notice of cancellation. In general it gives you three business days to cancel, or five if you're 65 or older. Make sure the notice is attached and read how to give notice. This is general information, not legal advice.

Q: What is a mechanics lien, and how do I protect myself?
A: If the builder doesn't pay its suppliers, subcontractors or workers, they can put a lien on your home. You could end up paying twice for the same work. Your contract has to include the mechanics lien warning. Ask for lien releases from the builder's suppliers and subs as you pay, and keep payments behind the work.

Q: The builder wants to charge extra for something mid-job. Is that allowed?
A: Changes have to be written up as a change order and signed by both of you before the extra work is done. A contract that is vague about rock, digging, allowances or deck area invites a large final bill, so pin those prices down before you sign. If a big extra shows up, ask for it in writing, with the price, before the crew goes ahead.

Q: Can someone review my pool bid before I sign?
A: Yes. Randy Beard, a licensed pool contractor and construction defect expert witness, reviews one residential pool bid for a flat $495. Q: Does a new pool need a permit, and who pulls it?
A: Yes, a new pool needs a building permit. The contractor usually pulls it, but the contract should say so in writing, along with who pays the permit and plan-check fees and any corrections the city requires. Keep the permit and the signed-off inspection card. They prove the pool is legal when you refinance or sell.

Q: What safety features does California require? Do I need a fence?
A: When a permit is issued for a new or remodeled pool or spa at a single-family home, California requires at least two of seven drowning-prevention features (Health and Safety Code section 115922). They include an isolation fence, removable mesh fencing, a safety cover, exit alarms on doors, self-closing latches on doors to the pool, and a water-entry alarm. An isolation fence has to be at least 60 inches tall, with a self-closing, self-latching gate that opens away from the pool. Your city may require more, so ask the builder which two features are in the price.

Q: Are pool drains dangerous? What should I look for?
A: A single suction outlet can hold a swimmer against it, so the codes require protection on every suction outlet. On a new pool that usually means split drains, two outlets far enough apart that one body can't block both. Drain covers have to be rated and they have an expiration date. If you have a pool and a spa, check both, because people often fix one and forget the other.

Q: Do I need HOA approval for a pool?
A: If you're in an HOA, almost certainly. Most CC&Rs require architectural approval before any exterior work, and a city permit doesn't replace it. Get the HOA's written approval before you sign with a builder, and make sure the plans you submit match what will be built. Check your own CC&Rs and architectural guidelines for the rules and the timeline.

Q: Which equipment brand should I get: Pentair, Hayward or Jandy?
A: All three make good pumps, filters, heaters and automation. The brand matters less than sizing the pump to the pipes, using a variable-speed pump, choosing the right heating approach, and picking a brand your local service companies stock and know. Whatever you choose, get every piece in the contract by make and model number. "Pump, filter, heater" is not a specification.

Q: Do I need a variable-speed pump?
A: In practice, yes. Federal efficiency rules and California's energy code make the filter pump on a new pool a variable-speed or multi-speed pump. The code also limits pump flow and requires the plumbing to be sized and laid out to cut pressure loss. A big pump on small pipe wears out early and wastes power, so ask the builder for pipe sizes, the pump size and the design flow.

Q: Did California ban gas pool heaters? Should I get a heat pump?
A: No, California did not ban gas pool heaters. Under the state energy code (Title 24, section 110.4(c)), though, a gas heater by itself is not one of the ways a new pool's heating system can pass. A common setup is a heat pump as the main heater, with gas as a supplement. Before you add a big gas heater, have the gas utility confirm the meter and line can supply it, and confirm the electrical service can carry a heat pump.

Q: Should I get a saltwater pool or regular chlorine?
A: A salt pool is still a chlorine pool. The salt cell makes chlorine from salt in the water, and many owners like the feel and not having to handle chlorine. Salt is harder on metal, natural stone and some equipment, and the cell needs cleaning and eventual replacement. On a new plaster finish, the plaster start-up guidelines say to hold off adding salt and running the cell for the first several weeks while the finish cures. Either way, the finish lasts or fails on balanced water, so follow your finish maker's chemistry ranges.

Q: Is pool automation worth it?
A: Automation is a convenience: it runs the pump schedule, heater, lights and sometimes chemical feeding from your phone. It only does what it's set up to do, though, so someone still needs to test the water and read the results. Bids often list automation, lighting, a cleaner and a cover as extras, so get each one priced and listed by model number.

Q: What is pool bonding, and why does the inspector care?
A: Bonding ties the pool's steel, the equipment, the lights and the metal around the pool together with copper so they all sit at the same electrical potential. That keeps swimmers from getting shocked. The electrical code requires it and the inspector checks it before it's covered up. Walkable decks and coping generally need a full bonding grid, not just a single wire, so ask the builder how the deck will be bonded.

Q: Plaster, quartz or pebble: which interior finish should I choose?
A: White plaster costs the least, quartz adds color and some durability, and pebble finishes generally cost the most and hold up the longest. More than the product itself, how long a finish lasts depends on how it was mixed and applied, the start-up, and keeping the water balanced afterward. Get the finish named by product in the bid, and ask what the finish warranty excludes as "water chemistry."

Q: What is plaster "start-up," and why does it matter?
A: Start-up is the brushing and water-chemistry routine during the first weeks after a new finish goes in. Most of the cement in the plaster cures during the first 28 days, and that care decides whether the finish spots, streaks, cracks or discolors. Two big ones: no acid washing a fresh finish to clear the haze, and no salt cell or chemical feeders until the start-up guidelines allow. Ask who does the start-up, for how long, and whether it's in the price.

Q: Should the pool be plastered before or after the deck and landscaping?
A: After, if you can. Fresh plaster is at its most vulnerable during its first few weeks. Plaster it before the deck, pavers and planting are done and it sits next to fertilizer, mulch, sawdust and wind-blown debris, all of which can stain it. If the schedule forces plaster to go in early, the contract should say who is responsible for staining from later work.

Q: How can I tell if my new pool is leaking?
A: Run an overnight test. In the evening, turn off the breakers to the pool equipment and shut off the auto-fill valve, because an auto-fill hides a leak by quietly topping the pool off. Mark and photograph the water line in the pool, the spa and any catch basin. If a level drops more than about a quarter inch overnight, you're losing water. Tell the builder in writing.
