# Your pool builder stopped showing up: the first 10 days

The crew has not been there in two weeks. The calls go to voicemail. You have paid half or more, and what you have is a hole with steel in it or a bare concrete shell. What you do in the next ten days has a lot to do with how much of your money you ever see again. This is the order Randy Beard, a licensed pool contractor and construction defect expert witness, would do things in if it were his own backyard.

## How this happens

The Contractors State License Board has a name for it: front-loading. Its own example is about pools. A builder asks for 50 percent of the price when the concrete shell is sprayed, has done about 15 percent of the work, and then leaves the job. In California a contractor may take a down payment of no more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. After that, a contractor may not request or accept a payment that exceeds the value of the work performed or the material delivered (Business and Professions Code section 7159.5). If your payments are ahead of the work, that is the first thing to know.

## Day 1: stop the money and freeze the site

  * **Stop paying.** Make no further payment until you know where you stand. If a lender is funding the job, tell the lender in writing today so it does not release another draw.
  * **Touch nothing.** No cleanup, no backfilling, no quick fix by a friend, and do not let anyone else touch it either.
  * **Start a written log.** The last day anyone worked, who was there, what was said. From here on, put everything in writing.



## Days 1 and 2: document what is actually there

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that decides the money later.

  * Photograph and video everything with the date showing: wide views first, then every wall, the floor, the steps and benches, the top edge of the shell, every pipe stub, the equipment pad, and the electrical and gas lines.
  * Put a tape measure in the picture.
  * If the steel is exposed, lay a ruler across it and photograph the spacing. Once concrete or plaster covers it, nobody can check it without drilling.
  * Measure the pool against the plan: length, width, depths, steps.
  * List the materials on site, and the materials you paid for that are not on site.
  * Make two piles of paper. Pile one: the signed contract, the plans, the payment schedule and every change order. Pile two: proof of every payment, front and back of every check.



What you paid against what is in the ground is the whole case.

## Days 1 to 3: make it safe, and watch the water

  * **Fence it now.** An open excavation is a fall hazard, and a shell that collects rainwater is a drowning hazard. Put up a sturdy temporary fence with a locked gate, and ask your building department what barrier it requires while the job is stopped.
  * **Tell your homeowner's insurance agent** there is an open excavation.
  * **Manage the water.** Rain softens the walls of an open dig. An empty concrete shell is light, and when the ground around it fills with water after heavy rain, the shell can be pushed up out of the ground. Keep surface water draining away from the hole, cap the open pipe ends, and get qualified advice before anyone pumps a shell dry in wet weather. A tarp is not a plan.



## Days 2 to 4: three lookups

  1. **The license.** [Look the builder up](https://capoolbuilders.com/) and write down the license status and classification, the bond company and bond number, and whether workers' compensation is on file. Screenshot it with the date. Every licensed contractor carries a $25,000 license bond. It is one bond shared by all of that contractor's claimants. It is not $25,000 for each job.
  2. **The permit.** Ask the building department who pulled the permit, which inspections passed, and when it expires. State law gives a permit 12 months, extensions come in periods of up to 180 days and must be requested in writing, and local rules vary. Ask for the expiration date in writing.
  3. **Who else is owed.** Go through your mail and pull every preliminary notice. Those are the subcontractors and suppliers who can have lien rights against your house.



## Can a subcontractor put a lien on my house if I already paid the builder?

Yes. The Contractors State License Board puts it this way: "Legally, the homeowner is ultimately responsible for payment — even if they already have paid the direct contractor." You can end up paying twice for the same work. There is also a clock. A job that simply stops is generally treated as complete after 60 days with no labor, and a subcontractor or supplier then generally has 90 days to record a lien (Civil Code sections 8180 and 8414). Ask in writing for lien releases for everything you have already paid for. Do not pay a subcontractor directly, and do not sign what a subcontractor hands you, until a construction attorney has looked at it. Have the attorney put the lien dates on a calendar from the last day anyone worked.

## Days 3 to 7: the letter, and how not to fire a pool contractor

Send the builder a calm, factual letter by regular mail and certified mail: what is unfinished, what you want done, and a firm date.

**Do not** text "you're fired." Do not lock the builder out. Do not hire a replacement yet. The contract has its own rules for notice and for a chance to fix the problem, and an owner who terminates the wrong way can become the party who broke the contract. This is the point to sit down with a California construction attorney, with both piles of paper. One question to ask: does the contract itself meet the state's requirements for a swimming pool contract? The law says a pool contract that does not substantially comply is "void and unenforceable by the contractor" (Business and Professions Code section 7167). What that means for you is the attorney's call.

## Days 5 to 10: start the remedies

  * **File a complaint with the Contractors State License Board.** It is free, you generally have four years, and it does not stop you from taking the legal route. Know its limits: the board itself says it cannot guarantee you get money back. It runs a free arbitration program that is mandatory for disputes of $25,000 or less and voluntary from $25,000 to $50,000.
  * **Open a claim with the bond company** named on the license record, and ask whether other claims are pending on the same bond. There is a deadline; ask the attorney.
  * **Get an independent evaluation before anyone covers the work.** It should produce three lists: work that is complete and acceptable, work that is defective, and work that was never finished. The cost to finish, compared with what is still unpaid on the contract, is the core of the claim. Steel, plumbing and the bonding wire get inspected and photographed before they are buried.
  * **Get replacement bids, not replacement work.** Written scopes from licensed pool contractors, based on that evaluation. [Check each bidder's license here](https://capoolbuilders.com/). Tell every one of them that nothing gets covered, demolished or changed until the documentation is finished.



## The five rules

  1. Stop paying the day the work stops.
  2. Photograph and measure everything before anyone touches it.
  3. Fence it, and manage the water.
  4. Find out who else is owed: preliminary notices and lien releases, or you can pay twice.
  5. Do not fire the builder, and do not let a new one cover the work, until an attorney and an independent evaluation say go.
